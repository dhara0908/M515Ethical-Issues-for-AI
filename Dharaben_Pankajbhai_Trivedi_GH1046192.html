Introduction¶

This project seeks to comment on the ethical issues that emerge due to AI and the use of the German Credit dataset to detect and minimise the bias in the AI systems. The process of credit risk assessment can be carried out in an automated manner that does not focus on or consider sensitive qualities such as sex and age, in which case the decision made would not be fair in the context of the credit. This report falls into a systematic approach, namely: Exploratory Data Analysis, Data Preprocessing, Fairness Baseline Model Development and Testing of Fairness, Fairness-Mitigation through a Fairness Awareness Algorithm. The outcomes must lead to fair forecasts, decision-making is made more transparent, and ethical, non-biased credit assessment recommendations are made.

Problem Statement¶

This project evaluates whether a machine-learning credit classification model produces unequal outcomes across sex groups, quantifies disparities using fairness metrics, and applies bias mitigation while assessing fairness-performance trade-offs.

Ethical Concerns¶

Predicting credit risk with machine learning poses serious ethical problems, mainly relating to fairness and unfairness. The credit data of the German has certain sensitive features (sex, age) which are also likely to influence the model output, even resulting in a biased tendency to a certain group in any prediction. An example is women or younger applicants who may be assigned a negative rating of their creditworthiness despite having economic indicators similar to the remaining applicants (Genovesi et al., 2024). Legal and reputational issues with financial institutions could arise out of this, irrespective of the ethical issues involved. The original data is not balanced on how the different groups of demographics and categories are represented and when learned by models, the imbalance in representation of the various groups might be even increased. Predictive accuracy can be maximised using machine learning algorithms, but at the same time, that can uphold social and ethical concerns in large measure, and may enhance systemic inequities unless carefully evaluated on these aspects. Moreover, opaque classifiers like the Random Forest or ensemble may even be difficult to interpret since the answer may not be clear as to why a particular individual is said to be at high risk. Transparency in the context of fair and responsible use of AI systems can be core to regulatory compliance, which in the case of the United Kingdom, the GDPR outlines as fairness, accountability, and explainability to inform AI-driven processes of decision-making (Sargeant, 2026). These are some areas of ethical concerns which demand the prior processing of the information, modelling the information with considerations of fairness and keeping a close check on the outcomes of the model on sensitive populations. Mitigation methods should be fair, maintain the performance of the model and be simple enough to articulate by the stakeholders.

This value indicates the model-trained baseline random forest has been trained with an example of bias mitigation with the Exponentiated Gradient algorithm of Fairlearn. This is constrained by the so-called Demographic Parity that is meant to achieve similar improvement in the prediction rate among the sexes (under the assumption that it is sex that is the sensitive feature). Equal training data (Xtrain, ytrain) with fairness is used to fit the model with adjustments that are also added in the fitting. The test set (y_pred_mitigated) is avoided and predictions are made on the test set which is then assessed. The Exponentiated Gradient algorithm works in a methodology, one tunes the classifier in an iterative process such that a certain level of accuracy is maintained to satisfy the demographic parity requirements (Almutairi, et al., 2024). This is one of the mitigations necessary in regard to societal and ethical application of artificial intelligence, particularly the use of artificial intelligence in financial operations where lending is discriminatory and not acceptable.

def false_discovery_rate(y_true, y_pred):
    tn, fp, fn, tp = confusion_matrix(
        y_true, y_pred, labels=[0, 1]
    ).ravel()

    denominator = fp + tp
    return fp / denominator if denominator != 0 else 0


def false_omission_rate(y_true, y_pred):
    tn, fp, fn, tp = confusion_matrix(
        y_true, y_pred, labels=[0, 1]
    ).ravel()

    denominator = fn + tn
    return fn / denominator if denominator != 0 else 0

Discussion¶

1. Accuracy¶

Accuracy is the ratio of the number of predictions the model got right to all the number of predictions it made. Base-line model has obtained overall accuracy of 74.33% (0.7433). Group 0 achieved 0.7423, while group 1 achieved 0.7438. The level of accuracy at the start of the test before extra training is taken is nearly the same across both groups at about 0.15 percentage points. There are similar overall predictive correctness levels for all the protected groups. But equality of accuracy does not equal fairness as there could be discrepancies in the nature of the errors and/or in the percentage of favourable predicting models.

2. Selection Rate¶

Selection rate defines ratio of applicants who are given the positive (1) prediction of the model. The baseline selection rate overall is 0.4833. Group 0 has a rate of 0.4433, compared with 0.5025 for group 1. Group 1 is 5.92 percentage points more likely to get positive predictions than group 0. Protected groups have different trajectories for positive outcomes in the model. Whether or not it is disadvantage in lending is very much dependent on what 1 class represents.

3. False Positive Rate (FPR)¶

FPR is the proportion of the applicants whose class is actually negative (0) but predicted as positive (1). The Overall Baseline FPR value is 0.2400. It is 0.2157 for group 0 and 0.2525 for group 1. For group 1, the FPR will be about 3.68 percentage points higher than group 0. Group 1 will be more often mislabeled as positive when it is actually negative. Depending on whether “class 1” is a positive or negative credit score, this is either a good and bad mistake respectively.

4. False Negative Rate (FNR)¶

FNR is the proportion of actually positive (1) cases that the model predicts to be negative (0). Overall baseline FNR is 0.2733. Group 0 has an FNR of 0.3043, while group 1 has 0.2596. The FNR of group 0 is around 4.48 percentage points higher. Applicants in group 0 who actually belong to the positive class, are more likely to be misclassified into the negative class. The answers to the questions in the Saving and Checking Account categories were updated with the most commonly given answer in the category, and categorical data were coded as numeric data to be analysed by machine learning algorithms (Biswas et al., 2025). If the favourable credit outcome is 1, then this would be a very significant potential disadvantage for group 0.

5. False Discovery Rate (FDR)¶

FDR is the rate of false positives out of the total number of positive predictions. Overall FDR is 0.2483. Group 0 has an FDR of 0.2558, compared with 0.2451 for group 1. The difference between the FRDs of these two groups is about 1.07 percentage points, with group 0 reporting a slightly higher FDR. The odds of a false positive is a touch higher for positive prediction of group 0 than of group 1. The gap is not that big, but it illustrates the fact that accuracy is not enough to explain group fairness.

6. False Omission Rate (FOR)¶

FOR is the percentage of negative predictions that are positive. Overall FOR is 0.2645. Group 0 records 0.2593, while group 1 records 0.2673. The FOR of group 1 is about 0.80 percentage points high compared to group 0. For applicants with negative predictions, there is no significant difference between the number of incorrect negative predictions between the two groups, though it is slightly higher for group 1.

7. Demographic Parity Difference (DPD)¶

DPD is a metric that compares the positive prediction rate of a group which is protected to another. The closer to 0, the more demographically similar. Baseline DPD is 0.0592. This is the difference between the selection rate from group 0 (0.4433) and group 1 (0.5025); or about 5.92 percentage points. There is some discrepancy in the rates of positive classifications between the protected groups in the baseline model. This shows quantitative empirical evidence of a demographic-parity gap, but does not, on its own, explain why there is a gap.

8. Demographic Parity Ratio / Disparate Impact¶

Demographic parity ratio is the ratio of the PPRs of the groups. Closer to 1 are more similar selection rates. The baseline demographic parity ratio of 0.8823 shows that they are roughly even in the demographic parity. The lower selection rate is around 88.2% of higher selection rate. There is differential distribution of positive outcomes among the protected groups. This measure may be used to numerically document the observed disparities, but should not be the only evidence of such disparity that is used to prove unlawful discrimination.

9. Equalized Odds Difference (EOD)¶

Equalized odds compares levels of error behaviour (such as a proportion of positives and negatives) between protected groups. A difference closer to 0 means that it is more alike. Baseline EOD is 0.0447. The relatively small value indicates that the baseline model's relevant error rates differ between groups, but the maximum difference is around 4.47 percentage points. The baseline model exhibits much more similar error-rate behaviour than perfect equality would necessitate yet burden of error is not equal across protected groups.

10. Results after mitigation¶

The results of the mitigation are especially significant because they demonstrate divergent movement of fairness across different definitions. Overall accuracy is reduced from 0.7433 to 0.7033 after mitigation. Group 0 accuracy falls from 0.7423 to 0.6289, whereas group 1 remains much higher at 0.7389. The pipeline is described as a focus on responsible use of AI, measuring and mitigating against bias in AI predictions, as well as any other pathway for ensuring transparency and accountability to financial institutions' decisions (Vishnubhatla, 2023). The selection rates are now rather similar: 0.5361 (group 0) and 0.5271 (group 1). Thus, the DPD value also improves significantly from 0.0592 to 0.0090 and the demographic parity ratio also improves from 0.8823 to 0.9832. In this study, biases were measured by recording measurements of fairness evaluation metrics, and this moral aspect was emphasized (Pagano et al., 2023). On the other hand, FPR varies, considerably, Group 0's FPR increases from 0.2157 to 0.4118, whereas group 1's becomes 0.2828. Thus, group 0 has a much higher rate of false-positive errors following mitigation. FNR becomes 0.3261 for group 0 and 0.2404 for group 1. FDR also becomes much higher for group 0 (0.4038) than group 1 (0.2617), while FOR is 0.3333 versus 0.2604. Most significantly, Equalized Odds Difference becomes worse, from 0.0447 to 0.1290, while the demographic parity improves.

Overall interpretation¶

The results should not be reported as “bias was removed”. Based on the evidence, however, a more sophisticated conclusion is possible: The mitigation procedure significantly reduced the difference in the positive prediction rates for the groups, but resulted in a loss of overall accuracy and a larger difference in some types of incorrect classifications. Therefore, since equalized odds decreases and demographic parity increases as a result of the intervention, demographic parity is worsening. This is a good argument for mistreatment disparate - a model should be able to do one fairness test better without necessarily passing the other fairness tests.

Limitations¶

The study uses a small dataset with demographic imbalance, missing-value imputation, limited protected attributes, and target-definition constraints. Fairness results also depend strongly on the selected metrics and mitigation approach.

Recommendations¶

Other in-processing mitigators, such as the Exponentiated Gradient method or other Fairness-aware methods, are recommended to continue for even better predictive power (while keeping the fairness). Other widely used pre-processing techniques available to boost model robustness are dealing with extreme credit amounts, and normalising skewed features (Akinjole et al., 2025). Provided that for sensitive variables (e.g. sex and age) the number of samples in each is balanced, retraining the model from time to time (at regular intervals) using new data can keep the model current and maintain the parity between the different variables. Combining with several fairness metrics enables monitoring and balancing of various aspects of ethical compliance.

Conclusion¶

The results found that predictions were slightly poorer with high-demographic-parity constraints; however, there was a significant improvement in the accuracy of prediction with the use of teaching fairness by sex. The overall experience with the pipeline reflects the difficulty of achieving high prediction accuracy while adhering to ethical principles and highlights the importance of ethical considerations in financial decision-making. In summary, findings from this research is actionable as outlined below and can assist companies in implementing AI in credit analysis, balancing regulatory and ethical concerns and business data standards to provide fair and transparent lending outcomes.
